## How we approach it

Automation projects fail in a specific, predictable way: someone automates the
process as described, then discovers the described process is not the real one.
The real one has exceptions, undocumented judgement calls, and a person who
quietly fixes things.

So we measure first. We sit with whoever does the work and count: how often,
how long, how many exceptions, and what happens when it goes wrong.

### The hard parts we take a position on

**Automate the measured process, not the documented one.** The gap between them
is where automation projects die. Finding it costs a few days; discovering it
after launch costs the project.

**Do not automate a bad process.** Making a broken workflow faster produces
broken results more quickly. Sometimes the finding is that the process should
change, and the software should be smaller than you expected.

**Leave a person in the loop where the cost of being wrong is high.** Full
automation is a good goal for rules and a bad goal for judgement. A confidence
threshold with human review above it is usually the right shape.
